Pathway variability, coat stiffening and mechanical adaptation during clathrin-mediated endocytosis

A visual guide to our preprint on how a single microscopic model — combining discrete stochastic assembly with continuum mechanics — explains the diversity of clathrin coat morphologies observed in nature: flat plaques, stalled pits, and closed vesicles.

What is clathrin even about?

Clathrin is a protein cells use to transport cargo (nutrients, other proteins, etc.) across the cellular membrane. It assembles into a highly regular hexagonal lattice that sometimes bends the membrane into a vesicle, enclosing the cargo. Clathrin coats are small, dynamic, and mechanically active. Experiments see flat lattices, stalled pits, and closed cages, but the path from local protein assembly to global membrane shape is still not fully understood. The structures are generally too small for live-cell imaging, and simulations are complicated as the system combines stochastic assembly, discrete geometry, and continuum mechanics.

This work

Assembly writes mechanics

Our model lets the material properties be generated by the coat itself during growth, allowing a selection of fates.

Dreckhoff, Schwarz & Lettermann, arXiv:2606.30267, 2026.

A minimal model respecting three main contributions

The model was built to include the three main contributors to complexity: stochastic protein assembly, discrete lattice geometry, and continuum membrane mechanics.

Assembly

Local stochastic events

Individual clathrin-like units bind to the membrane, diffuse, form contacts, break contacts, and leave again.

Geometry

A discrete lattice

Those local events build a connected network with pentagons, hexagons, defects, and connectivity constraints, all on a curved surface.

Mechanics

A deformable membrane

The coat grows on a spherical-cap membrane whose curvature can either be imposed or is allowed to adapt.

From an experimental coat to a computational one

The simulation starts from the same visual object seen in microscopy: a clathrin coat sitting on a membrane. We coarse-grain the coat into clathrin-like agents connected by elastic legs and describe the membrane as a spherical cap.

This is deliberately minimal. The goal is not to simulate every molecular detail, but to ask whether local assembly rules alone can create a mechanical object that chooses a fate.

Clathrin Coats Stiffen and Remember their Curvature During Growth

The first scenario is coat growth at fixed curvature. This is the equivalent of coat growth on fixed beads on cells. The advantage is that this decouples membrane deformations from coat development, easing interpretation.

Two Material Properties Emerge

As closure – the ratio of occupied area to total area of a sphere at a given curvature – increases, the lattice becomes harder to bend. At the same time, the preferred curvature of the entire coat shifts away from the microscopically preferred curvature. The first effect is due to residual stresses forming when a spherical cap is deformed that cannot be relaxed, activating the bulk modulus. As the bulk modulus is much higher than the bending modulus, this effectively stiffens the coat. The second effect, which we name Curvature Memory, is due to clathrin being incorporated relaxed at its growth curvature, thereby carrying a record of its growth history.

  • Coat stiffening: sufficiently connected, more curved coats resist curvature changes much more strongly.
  • Curvature memory: the effective preferred curvature of the entire coat shifts toward the curvature present during assembly.

If the Membrane is Allowed to Adapt, Three Fates Emerge

Once membrane curvature is allowed to respond to the total system energy, the same microscopic assembly rules branch into the main morphologies seen in experiments.

Pathway variability

The outcome is not imposed in advance. Each trajectory begins as a small, nearly flat assembly. Depending on connectivity, membrane parameters, and growth history, it can stay flat, bend and stall, or continue toward closure.

Flat plaque

The coat grows in area but never becomes mechanically coherent enough to transmit tresses and stiffen enough.

Aborted pit

The coat bends the membrane, but stalls before reaching full closure in limited time.

Closed vesicle

The system continues to bend and achieves closure.

Two gates control the final fate

The simulations point to two decisions, one controlled by discrete assembly and one controlled by mechanics after the coat has started to behave as a connected material.

An energy landscape for growing coats

The landscape asks where growth is energetically easy. Polymerisation pushes coats toward larger occupied area, membrane bending penalizes curvature, and clathrin bending favors the curvature stored in the coat.

Fates emerge because growth changes which part of this landscape is accessible. Early coats can stay near the flat route; later coats may develop a finite-curvature minimum that pulls the system toward invagination or closure.

Gate 1

Connectivity

The coat must become connected enough to transmit stress across the lattice.

Mechanics

Geometry-induced stiffening

Once stress can propagate, bending a curved lattice forces small dilations that excite the costly stretching mode.

Gate 2

Growth history

The moving energy minimum decides whether the coat stalls as an aborted pit or keeps moving toward closure.

How the gates read physically

Early on, polymerization favors rapid, almost flat growth. If the lattice stays loosely connected, that flat route remains energetically easy. Once connectivity crosses a threshold, clathrin bending begins to dominate and a finite-curvature minimum appears.

The second gate is later and more continuous: membrane tension, coat stiffness, and curvature memory determine whether the system can catch the energy minimum as an aborted pit or is pulled toward the line of full closure.

Where the gates sit in the simulated landscape

The same logic can be mapped back onto the curvature-area plane used throughout the model. Flat, aborted, and closed outcomes occupy distinct regions, and the gates describe how a growing coat moves between them.

Why stiffness and memory arise

The fixed-curvature and adaptive-curvature simulations point to the same mechanism: growth turns a flexible assembly into a mechanical object with history-dependent material properties.

Geometry couples bending to stretching

The theory turns the simulation result into a geometric statement. Bending a curved coat to a higher curvature cannot be achieved by pure bending alone; it also creates compression and dilation in the lattice. Because clathrin resists stretching much more strongly than bending, the effective bending rigidity rises as the coat grows.

Curvature memory follows from the same idea in time: each newly incorporated clathrin is relaxed at the curvature where it joined. The preferred curvature of the whole coat is therefore a weighted mixture of microscopic preference and growth history.

Cutting reveals stored stress

If an assembled coat stores mechanical stress, cutting it should release that stress and let the remaining lattice relax. The simulations reproduce this kind of mechanical response.
