How Cells Pull Things In

A sixty-year story of clathrin, curvature, and a question only just being answered

Every one of your cells constantly pulls material in from the outside world. To do it, the cell wraps tiny patches of its own surface into bubbles and swallows them. The machine that builds these bubbles is a protein called clathrin, which assembles into a soccer-ball-like cage that bends a flat membrane into a sphere.

How a flat sheet decides to curve into a ball — and why it sometimes fails — is a puzzle scientists have chased for sixty years. Here's the story.

Click any milestone to expand it. Read along the snake: left to right, then back again.

  1. 1
    1964

    The first glimpse

    +

    Roth & Porter, peering into mosquito cells with an electron microscope, spot strange "bristle-coated" pits forming at the cell surface and pinching off into the cell. Nobody yet knows what the bristly coat is made of — but they correctly guess that these pits are how the cell takes things in.

    Roth, T.F. & Porter, K.R., J. Cell Biol., 1964.
  2. 2
    1969

    "A vesicle in a basket"

    +

    Kanaseki & Kadota describe the coat's striking geometry: a lattice of hexagons and pentagons, like a tiny geodesic basket cradling each vesicle. The shape itself hints that the coat is what curves the membrane.

    Kanaseki, T. & Kadota, K., J. Cell Biol., 1969.
  3. 3
    1973

    The physics of bending membranes

    +

    In parallel, physicist Wolfgang Helfrich writes down the mathematics of how thin membranes resist bending — treating the cell membrane like an elastic sheet with a stiffness and a preferred shape. This framework becomes the language for everything that follows.

    Helfrich, W., Z. Naturforsch. C, 1973.
  4. 4
    1975–76

    Naming clathrin

    +

    Barbara Pearse purifies these coated vesicles and discovers that a single protein builds the entire cage. She names it clathrin, from the Latin for "lattice." The central player finally has a name.

    Purification: Pearse, B.M.F., J. Mol. Biol., 1975. Naming & hexagon/pentagon network description: Pearse, B.M.F., PNAS, 1976.
  5. 5
    1980

    Catching the cage in the act

    +

    John Heuser uses rapid-freezing electron microscopy to capture clathrin lattices frozen mid-assembly on the membrane, seeing flat sheets and curved baskets side by side. This raises the question at the heart of the field: how does a flat lattice become a curved one?

    Heuser, J., J. Cell Biol., 1980.
  6. 6
    ~1990s

    Mapping the machine

    +

    Through the work of Tom Kirchhausen and many others, the molecular cast assembles: the triskelion (clathrin's three-legged building block), the adaptor proteins that recruit it, and the picture of clathrin as a self-assembling, programmable cage. The parts list is now known — but the mechanism of bending is still debated.

    e.g. Ungewickell & Branton, Nature, 1981; Kirchhausen, T., Annu. Rev. Cell Dev. Biol., 1999.
  7. 7
    2018

    Flat or curved from the start?

    +

    A long-running controversy: does clathrin first lay down a flat sheet and then bend it, or does it curve as it grows? Bucher et al. and others show that the balance of clathrin and adaptors tips the outcome, reframing the flat-to-curved transition as something tunable rather than fixed.

    Bucher, D. et al., Nat. Commun., 2018.
  8. 8
    2021

    The coat is under tension

    +

    Two key experiments. Sochacki et al. map the spontaneous curvature built into flat lattices, and Tagiltsev et al. perform a remarkable trick: they cut a clathrin lattice and watch it spring to a tighter curl — proving the coat is an elastically loaded spring, storing mechanical energy. The coat doesn't just sit there; it pushes.

    Sochacki, K.A. et al., Dev. Cell, 2021; Tagiltsev, G. et al., eLife, 2021.
  9. 9
    2023

    Watching curvature happen, live

    +

    Mund et al. use super-resolution microscopy to follow individual coats bending in real cells, supporting a "cooperative" picture where assembly and curvature progress together. The flat-to-curved question is now answerable with data — but a unifying physical explanation is still missing.

    Mund, M. et al., Nat. Cell Biol., 2023.
  10. 10
    This work

    One model, three fates

    +

    This paper builds a computer simulation of a growing clathrin coat from the bottom up and finds something surprising: curvature doesn't need a special trigger. As the cage grows, its own geometry makes it progressively stiffer, and this stiffening alone can drive a flat sheet to curl into a sphere.

    The very same rules spontaneously produce all three outcomes seen in cells — flat plaques, stalled "aborted" pits, and fully sealed vesicles — depending only on how the coat grows. It even reproduces the spring-back seen in the 2021 cutting experiments. A sixty-year-old question, answered by geometry.

    Read the paper → Dreckhoff, J.H.H., Schwarz, U.S. & Lettermann, L., arXiv:2606.30267, 2026.
